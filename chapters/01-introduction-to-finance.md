# Chapter 1 — Introduction to Finance
*What the field is actually for, and why the central claim is harder than it sounds.*

---

## The puzzle of Bacon Signs

In 1978, a regional sign company in the Midwest discovered it was better at making signs than the national franchise companies were — better materials, better craftsmen, better result. The nationals noticed. They started sending subcontract work. Demand grew faster than reputation.

Here is where most accounts of the story stop. The business was succeeding; what more is there to say?

What more there is to say is this: **demand exceeded what Bacon Signs could finance.** New welders, new equipment, bigger work bays — these had to be paid *before* the cash from the nationals came back through the door, and those nationals took ninety days to pay. A firm that cannot pre-fund its growth cannot accept its growth.

Bacon Signs got past that constraint. The owner, the banker, and the firm's vice president of finance built a plan in advance — named when capital would be needed, how much, what kind, from whom.[^1] Same craftsmen, same signs. Different machinery underneath.

That gap between the business succeeding and the business being able to sustain its success is exactly what finance is about. Not signs. Not the Midwest. The general thing.

[^1]: Dun & Bradstreet, "Bacon Signs, Inc.", D&B Business Directory.

## What "finance" actually means

Let me start with the definition, and then immediately explain why the definition is not enough.

**Finance is the study of how cash moves between people and firms over time, and how to make consistently good decisions about that movement when the future is uncertain.**

That sentence is correct. It is also doing three different jobs at once without telling you, and a student who learns the definition without learning the split will be confused for the next thirteen weeks.

Here are the three jobs.

**Business finance** is what a manager inside a firm does with the firm's money. Should we buy the new machine? Borrow or sell stock? Extend credit or insist on cash? Pay a dividend or reinvest? These decisions are made by people whose job is to maximize the value of the firm — a phrase that sounds simple and, as Chapter 2 will show, is doing enormous work.

**Investments** is what a person or institution does with money they already have when they want it to grow. Buying stocks, buying bonds, building portfolios. This is the visible face of finance — the cable-news ticker, the *Billions* characters — but most of the actual work is patient analysis of cash flows under uncertainty.

**Financial markets and institutions** is the infrastructure layer: the exchanges, the regulators, the banks. Without it, the first two jobs cannot happen. A Bacon Signs cannot meet its banker. An investor cannot buy a share of stock.

The three areas overlap constantly. A bank makes business-finance decisions about its own balance sheet while offering investment products to customers while being regulated by the infrastructure layer. The split is not a wall — it is a way of telling which kind of question you are asking.

Most finance graduates, for most of their careers, work in business finance. Not investments. Not the glamorous part. The CFO's office, the treasurer's team, the controller's function. This is worth saying plainly, because the TV shows do not say it.

## Three terms that need unpacking

Three words appear in the definition and are doing more work than they look like they are doing. Finance gets sloppy around all three, and the sloppiness costs you later.

**Money.** In conversation, money means cash. In finance, money means *purchasing power transferred over time* — the ability to claim resources now or later. A dollar in your hand is money. A government bond promising you a dollar a year from now is also money in this sense — same purchasing power, different timing, with risk attached. Most of finance is the art of translating between the two.

**Risk.** Not danger. Not even bad outcomes. **Uncertainty about the size and timing of future cash flows — including outcomes better than expected as well as worse.** A stock with returns ranging from −30% to +50% in any given year is *more* risky than a Treasury bill yielding exactly 2% — because the uncertainty is wider, in both directions. Finance, when it is doing its job, makes risk a measurable thing, not a feeling.

**Value.** At least three meanings, depending on who is talking.

- *Book value* — what accountants record on the balance sheet, based on historical cost adjusted for depreciation. Backward-looking.
- *Market value* — what the asset trades for right now. Forward-looking, because the price reflects what buyers expect about the future.
- *Economic value* — what someone would actually pay if you cornered them honestly. Often higher than market value, because markets aggregate willingness-to-pay and most buyers get a slight discount on what they would have agreed to.

When a financial argument seems contradictory, check first whether two different "values" are being mashed together in the same sentence. The contradiction usually dissolves.

## The three decisions inside business finance

Inside business finance — the first job — a manager makes three kinds of decisions, and only three. Everything that happens in a corporate finance function eventually maps to one of them.

<!-- → [TABLE: Three finance decisions mapped to balance sheet regions — columns: Decision type, What it covers, Where it lives on the balance sheet, Bacon Signs example] -->

**Working capital management.** Short-term assets and short-term liabilities. Should we extend net-30 credit to this customer or net-60? When a supplier offers a discount for paying within ten days, do we take it? How much cash should we hold versus invest? These decisions sit at the top of the balance sheet.

**Capital budgeting.** Long-term assets. Should we acquire the new factory? Replace the truck fleet? Launch the new product line? These are the high-stakes decisions, because the investments are large and reversal is expensive. The entire machinery of Chapters 15 and 16 is built to answer one question: is this long-term project actually worth what it costs?

**Capital structure.** Long-term financing — the right side of the balance sheet, below the working-capital line. Debt or equity? Issue bonds or sell stock? Reinvest earnings or pay them out? The choice changes who owns the firm, who has a claim on its cash flows, and how much of the firm's risk is absorbed by which class of investor.

Now pull Bacon Signs apart with those three buckets.

When Bacon accepted ninety-day payment terms from the nationals, it tied up cash in receivables for three months at a time — it needed a credit line to fund payroll and materials during that gap. *Working capital.* When it added a second work bay and new welding equipment, the owner had to estimate whether the cash flows from new capacity would justify the cost of the equipment. *Capital budgeting.* When the financing plan chose bank debt over outside equity — keeping family ownership intact at the cost of higher leverage — that was a deliberate trade. *Capital structure.*

Same firm, same growth, three different decisions, each in a different region of the balance sheet.

## The central trade-off

Here is the sentence the entire field of finance is built on. I am going to give it to you exactly, and then show you what happens to it when it gets misquoted — which is almost always.

> Investments with greater risk should, *on average and over time*, deliver greater expected returns.

Notice the words that get dropped.

**"On average."** A risky investment can lose money in any given year. The claim is about long-run averages across many investments or many years, not about any one outcome.

**"Over time."** Risk premiums need decades to show up reliably. Five years is not enough time to prove that stocks beat bonds, even though across nearly a century they have.

**"Should."** This is a prediction the theory makes, not a guarantee the universe provides. Asset prices can wander away from the trade-off for long stretches. When the data and the claim disagree, finance treats that as a puzzle to investigate, not as a sign the claim is wrong.

Why does the trade-off have to exist at all?

Suppose two investments offered identical expected returns — say, 6% per year — but one had wildly variable outcomes (sometimes −20%, sometimes +35%) and the other was a steady 6% every year. Almost everyone picks the steady one. *Risk-averse* is the technical term for this preference, and it is the empirical fact that makes the whole apparatus work. If risk-averse investors won't take a risky bet for the same expected return as a safe one, then risky investments have to offer *more* expected return to attract buyers at all. The size of that "more" — the **risk premium** — is what assets compete on.

The trade-off exists not because the universe rewards bravery, but because the price of risky things has to fall until risk-averse investors are willing to hold them. A lower price means a higher expected return going forward. The premium is built into the price.

## What risk is made of

The word "risk" hides several different sources of uncertainty. The split matters because some types can be reduced by holding many investments together, and some cannot.

**Default risk** — the issuer fails to make the promised payment. A corporate bond can default. A startup can go to zero. The probability of default is part of why two bonds of identical maturity can offer different yields.

**Inflation risk** — your cash flows arrive on schedule, but their purchasing power has shrunk. Lock yourself into a 3% bond when inflation runs at 5% and you are losing real wealth even though no one defaulted. Chapter 3 will spend serious time here.

**Diversifiable (unsystematic) risk** — the part of an individual stock's variability that is specific to that company. A new CEO, a botched product launch, a lawsuit. Hold one stock and you bear all of it. Hold a hundred stocks and the company-specific shocks largely cancel out — the good ones and the bad ones average toward zero across the portfolio.

**Non-diversifiable (systematic) risk** — the part of variability that *cannot* be diversified away because it affects the whole market. Recessions, financial crises, broad shifts in interest rates. The standard measure for a stock's exposure to systematic risk is **beta**, which we will build from scratch in Chapter 14. The crucial point now: diversifiable risk should not be priced, because you can avoid it for free by holding a portfolio. Systematic risk should be priced — investors deserve compensation for risk they cannot escape.

**Political risk** — the rules of the game change. Tax policy, regulation, expropriation. Hard to model with the same equations as the others; named separately because it can dominate everything else in some regions and industries.

## A worked example: what does 6% actually mean?

You have $10,000 to invest for thirty years, and two choices.

- **Option A** — A US Treasury inflation-protected bond yielding 2% real, guaranteed by the federal government.
- **Option B** — A diversified equity index fund with an expected real return of 6% per year, but with a standard deviation of returns around 17%.

Run the arithmetic on Option A:

$$10{,}000 \times (1.02)^{30} \approx \$18{,}114$$

Predictable, modest, more than doubling but only barely.

Run the central case for Option B:

$$10{,}000 \times (1.06)^{30} \approx \$57{,}435$$

Three times Option A. That is the risk premium, *if it shows up.* But Option B's standard deviation tells you it might not show up cleanly. In any given year, Option B can be down 25% or up 40%. Over thirty years, you can land anywhere from roughly $25,000 to $130,000 with 80% probability — assuming returns behave the way the historical data suggest.

<!-- → [CHART: Fan chart showing distribution of possible 30-year outcomes for Option B, with Option A's $18,114 as a flat line for reference — student should see the width of the cone and the relationship between the expected value and the guaranteed value] -->

The trade-off is real, but it is *probabilistic*. You give up the certainty of $18,114 to get an expected value of $57,435 with a wide range of possible outcomes. Finance gives you tools to make the trade quantitatively. It does not make the trade for you.

## Where finance happens

The first two sections told you what finance studies and what trade-off it is built on. This one tells you where finance physically happens.

### Primary versus secondary markets

The cleanest distinction in the field. **Primary market** transactions are sales of *new* securities — the issuing firm or government receives the cash. **Secondary market** transactions are sales of *previously issued* securities between investors — the issuer is no longer involved and receives nothing.

The analogy to new versus used cars goes only so far. What the analogy does not capture is this: secondary-market trading prices feed information back into the primary market. Trading a share of Apple between two investors does not send cash to Apple, but it changes the price at which Apple could issue more stock tomorrow. Markets are information machines as much as they are exchange machines.

When a firm goes public for the first time, that is an **initial public offering (IPO)** — primary market. Every trade after the initial transaction is secondary.

### Money markets and capital markets

A maturity-based split. **Money markets** trade short-term, low-risk, highly liquid debt instruments with maturities of one year or less: Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, overnight federal funds. **Capital markets** trade longer-term instruments — bonds and stocks — where maturities run from years to decades, and stocks carry no maturity at all.

### Three types of players

**A broker** brings buyers and sellers together and earns a commission. The broker does not own the security; they facilitate. They take no risk on the trade.

**A dealer** owns inventory of the security and stands ready to buy from you at a *bid* price or sell to you at an *ask* price. The dealer earns the **spread** — the difference between bid and ask — multiplied by trading volume. Dealers "make markets." Their willingness to quote both sides at all times is what makes a security liquid enough to trade.

**A financial intermediary** transforms one kind of financial claim into another. A commercial bank takes your deposits — short-term, insured, liquid — and uses them to make mortgage loans — long-term, secured, illiquid. The intermediary is not just facilitating someone else's trade; it is creating a new financial product, and absorbing the mismatch between what depositors want and what borrowers need.

That mismatch absorption matters. If a borrower defaults on a mortgage held by your bank, the bank absorbs the loss; your deposit is unaffected. If you had bought a mortgage-backed security directly, you would absorb the loss yourself. Intermediation moves risk around. Where it gets moved to is one of the most consequential questions in the field.

### A single trade, traced end to end

When you tap "Buy 1 share of AAPL" on a brokerage app, here is what actually happens.

Your brokerage receives the order and routes it to a market-making firm. That firm — a **dealer** — holds inventory of AAPL shares and quotes a bid (say, $189.50) and an ask ($189.52). You buy at the ask. The dealer earns the $0.02 spread. The exchange records the trade. A clearinghouse settles it. Two days later, the share appears in your account.

Apple receives nothing. Its outstanding share count is unchanged. This is **secondary market** activity — you bought from whoever previously held the share, via the dealer as intermediary, and Apple sat entirely outside the transaction.

If instead you had bought a share at Apple's 1980 IPO, the cash would have flowed to Apple itself. That was **primary market** — Apple issued a new share and received the proceeds. Every trade since then has been secondary, and Apple has collected nothing from any of them.

## Finance as a way of seeing

Three concepts, one shape.

Finance is the study of trade-offs over time under uncertainty. Every chapter that follows is an instance of this. Time value of money (Chapters 7–9) gives you the arithmetic for trading cash now against cash later. Bond and stock valuation (Chapters 10–11) applies that arithmetic to specific instruments. Statistics and regression (Chapters 13–14) build the tools for measuring uncertainty. Capital budgeting (Chapters 15–16) applies the trade-off to long-term investment decisions. Capital structure (Chapter 17) applies it to financing choices. Forecasting and working capital (Chapters 18–19) bring it to short-term planning. Risk management (Chapter 20) is the discipline of choosing which trade-offs to take and which to hedge.

Underneath each chapter is the same skeleton: cash flows happen at different times, with different probabilities, and we need a way to make them commensurable so we can decide. Finance is the field that decided to take that problem seriously.

The other thing to notice — because this book is *Principles of Finance with LLMs* — is that large language models are now a real part of how this work gets done. A junior analyst in 2026 builds a discounted cash-flow model with Claude checking assumptions, drafting alternative scenarios, translating accounting language into plain English, spotting inconsistencies in a 10-K. The finance is still finance. The tools are different. Chapter 00 is about using these tools well, which mostly means knowing where they help, where they hurt, and how to tell the difference.

## What would change my mind

The chapter argues finance is best framed as the study of trade-offs over time under uncertainty, with the risk-return trade-off as the central organizing claim. The reading would have to revise if the empirical risk-return relationship turned out to be reliably backwards — if low-risk asset classes systematically outperformed high-risk ones over multi-decade periods across multiple economies. Some narrow reversals exist (low-volatility anomalies, Japan's lost decades), and the chapter acknowledges them. A wholesale reversal would mean the central claim is wrong in a deep way, and the field would need a new organizing frame.

## Still puzzling

The fuzziest piece of this chapter is the boundary between *risk* — uncertainty where probability distributions are known — and *uncertainty* in the deeper sense Frank Knight named in 1921, where we cannot even put a distribution on outcomes. The textbook frames everything as risk because the math works on risk. Knightian uncertainty is what the models stop being able to handle. The 2008 crisis, the COVID dislocations of 2020, the LTCM blowup of 1998 — at the moments that matter most, the assumption that we have a probability distribution starts looking like the assumption doing the most damage. I don't have a clean way to teach this in a first chapter without spooking the reader. I've left it for the synthesis chapter at the end of the book, but it nags.
