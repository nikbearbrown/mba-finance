## Specifications (pulling apart vague terms)

- **"Money"** — purchasing power transferred over time, not just cash.
- **"Risk"** — uncertainty about size and timing of future cash flows, including better-than-expected outcomes; not danger.
- **"Value"** — at least three meanings: book (historical), market (forward-looking trading price), economic (willingness to pay). Most contradictions in finance arguments dissolve when you check which "value" is being used.
- **"Risk-free"** — shorthand for default-risk-free in nominal terms; ignores inflation, reinvestment, interest-rate risk.
- **"Higher risk means higher return"** — drops three words: *expected*, *on average*, *over time*. The dropped words are where the field actually lives.

## Mechanism explanations (reusable)

- **Why risk-averse preferences make the risk-return trade-off exist.** If risk-averse investors won't take a risky bet at the same expected return as a safe one, risky asset prices have to fall until expected returns are higher. The trade-off is enforced by *price*, not by cosmic fairness.
- **The four sources of risk decomposition:** default, inflation, diversifiable (unsystematic), non-diversifiable (systematic). Plus political risk as a separately named category.
- **Diversification eliminates company-specific risk for free, so it shouldn't be priced.** Only systematic risk earns a premium. Reusable backbone for Ch 13–14.

## Worked-example structures

- **The "$10,000 over 30 years at 2% vs. 6%"** comparison — clean way to show what a 4-percentage-point risk premium produces over a long horizon. Endpoints: $18,114 vs. $57,435. Reusable in any TVM chapter.
- **"Tap to buy 1 share of AAPL"** — five-step trace from order entry to settlement, showing broker → dealer → exchange → clearinghouse → account. Reusable for Ch 11 (stocks), Ch 17 (raising capital).

## Analogies (use with care)

- **Primary vs. secondary market = new cars vs. used cars.** Limit: doesn't capture how secondary-market prices feed information back into primary issuance. Name the limit when you use it.
- **Brokers, dealers, intermediaries as three different jobs at the same desk.** Brokers = matchmakers. Dealers = inventory holders. Intermediaries = claim transformers.

## Three-region balance-sheet mnemonic

- Working-capital management → top of the balance sheet (current assets, current liabilities)
- Capital budgeting → left side, long-term assets
- Capital structure → right side, long-term financing mix

Useful diagrammatic anchor for Ch 5 (financial statements) and Ch 16 (capital budgeting).

## Quotable phrasings

- "Finance is the study of how cash moves between people and firms over time, and how to make consistently good and repeatable decisions about that movement when the future is uncertain."
- "Markets are information machines as much as they are exchange machines."
- "The trade-off is enforced by price, not by cosmic fairness."
- "There is no decision in business finance that does not look, when you squint at it, like a trade between *being able to react quickly* and *earning more over time.*"

## Counter-arguments and limits to flag forward

- **Knightian uncertainty.** Risk has a probability distribution; uncertainty doesn't. The chapter flags this for the synthesis chapter at the end of the book.
- **Equity returns of the 2000s, Japan since 1990.** Risk-return trade-off can fail across multi-decade windows. Useful for Ch 12, Ch 15.
- **2008 disintermediation and re-regulation.** Worth returning to in Ch 17 and Ch 20.
