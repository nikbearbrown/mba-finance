## Specifications

- **"Interest rate"** — the rental price of money. Determined by supply, demand, expected inflation, and risk premium.
- **"Inflation"** — at least four measures: CPI, core CPI, PPI, GDP deflator. Each answers a different question.
- **"GDP"** — market value of output. Real strips out inflation; nominal does not.
- **"Risk-free"** rate — risk-free in nominal terms over short horizons. Long-horizon real-return holders still bear inflation risk.
- **"Recession"** — popularly two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP; officially whatever NBER says.

## Mechanism explanations (reusable)

- **Why nominal vs. real matters.** Same nominal rate (12%) is much cheaper in a 10% inflation world than 6% nominal in a 1% inflation world. Reusable in Ch 7 (TVM), Ch 10 (bonds), Ch 17 (cost of capital).
- **The risk-premium ladder.** Treasury → IG corporate → HY corporate → mortgage → credit card. Spread reflects credit risk. Reusable in Ch 10.
- **Why headline unemployment understates labor-market slack.** Discouraged workers leave the labor force; U-3 falls without anyone finding work. Reusable in Ch 18.
- **Three flavors of currency exposure.** Transaction, translation, economic. Reusable in Ch 20.

## Worked-example structures

- **The "real cost of debt" computation** — pull nominal rate from 10-K debt footnote, subtract inflation. Compares 1980s analog to 2010s analog cleanly. Reusable in Ch 17.
- **FRED workflow for a homebuilder** — three-series query (mortgage rate, housing starts, existing sales) + segment data → macro analysis chapter. Template for the project.
- **Japan GDP percentage change** — Q1→Q2 2013, exact number from BEA via FRED. Pedagogical example for the percentage-change formula.

## Analogies

- **Interest rate = rental price of money.** Limit: doesn't capture term structure or risk premium; treats money as a homogeneous good when it isn't.
- **Inflation as a "tax" on cash holdings.** Limit: it's not a literal tax — no government collects it — but the wealth-transfer effect is real.
- **NBER dates recessions; doesn't predict them.** *Documenting* the cycle, not forecasting.

## Counter-arguments and limits

- **The Phillips curve's failure modes.** Useful but not deterministic; 1970s and 2022 cycles have stress-tested it.
- **GDP doesn't measure wellbeing.** Important caveat for any policy-adjacent chapter.
- **Soft-landing debate (2024–2025).** Useful for teaching calibrated uncertainty; chapter exercise 3.9 is built around it.
- **Real-time data revisions.** FRED data points get revised — the most recent quarter is preliminary.
